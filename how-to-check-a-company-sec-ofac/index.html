Practical public-data guide

How to check a company using SEC and OFAC public data

A useful company check starts with a precise identity, reviews relevant public records, and records what the evidence does—and does not—support.

Important scope limitThis process is a bounded public-data screen. It is not legal advice, a credit decision, compliance certification, comprehensive due diligence, full KYB, or a guarantee that a company is safe.

A five-step public-data check

  1. Identify the company precisely

    Start with the legal name, known trading names, jurisdiction, website, and any reliable registration or filing identifier. Similar names can refer to different organizations, so identity resolution comes before conclusions.

  2. Review relevant SEC company records

    Use the SEC’s official EDGAR filing search to look for a company name, ticker, or CIK. Review the filing entity and dates—not just a search-result headline. EDGAR covers public companies and other filers; absence from EDGAR does not prove that a private company is invalid.

  3. Search OFAC sanctions data

    Use OFAC’s official Sanctions List Search tool for the company name and relevant aliases. OFAC describes its name search as using fuzzy logic, so a possible match requires review of identifiers and context. A similar name is not automatically the same entity.

  4. Compare the evidence across sources

    Check whether names, locations, identifiers, and dates are consistent. Separate confirmed facts from plausible matches and unresolved gaps. Do not turn a missing record into a positive claim.

  5. Document the decision and its limits

    Record the sources checked, the date, the evidence found, and any ambiguity. A defensible outcome may be “insufficient evidence” when the available public data cannot support a stronger conclusion.

What SEC and OFAC checks can tell you

  • Whether a searched entity appears to have relevant SEC filings under the identity reviewed.
  • Whether a searched name produces potential matches in selected OFAC sanctions data.
  • Whether important identity details agree or conflict across the reviewed evidence.
  • Which gaps require more information or a different source.

What these checks cannot establish

  • That a company is legally compliant, financially sound, trustworthy, or safe.
  • That a company has no sanctions exposure merely because one name search returned no match.
  • Beneficial ownership, licensing, litigation, creditworthiness, or all jurisdiction-specific records.
  • A complete KYB or due-diligence conclusion.

Why human review matters

Automated searches can surface useful evidence, but company names, aliases, transliterations, addresses, and changing records create ambiguity. A reviewer should verify that the evidence refers to the intended company, examine potential sanctions matches, and identify when the available evidence is insufficient.

Frequently asked questions

Does an SEC search cover every company?

No. SEC EDGAR includes public-company filings and filings from other covered filers. A private company may have no relevant SEC record.

Does no OFAC name match prove a company is safe?

No. It describes only the searched data and inputs. It is not a safety guarantee, legal conclusion, or complete due-diligence result.

What if the evidence is ambiguous?

Record the uncertainty, obtain better identifiers, and avoid a stronger conclusion than the evidence supports.
